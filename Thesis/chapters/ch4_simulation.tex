\chapter{Simulation Design and Data}
\label{ch:simulation}

The idea behind Part~II is simple: one historical stream of orders is replayed
twice, once through a continuous engine and once through a batch engine.
Because both engines receive exactly the same events, everything that could
otherwise distort the comparison is held fixed, and the matching engine is the
only thing that differs.

\section{Simulation Environment}
\label{sec:environment}

\subsection{The Two Engines and the Replay}

Both engines are written in Rust and work on the same order and trade format,
so a difference in results cannot come from how orders are represented. They
are otherwise independent programs with no common interface. A shared replay
program reads the event stream once, in time order, and gives every order to
\emph{both} engines in the same pass, which is what makes a comparison
interval by interval possible. The metrics are computed by one shared piece of
code outside the two engines, so both are measured in exactly the same way.
The replay follows the timestamps stored in the data, not the computer's
clock, so the results do not depend on how fast the machine runs. It is
deterministic, because nothing in either engine is random.

\subsection{The Continuous Engine (CDA)}

The continuous engine keeps a limit order book in which orders are served by
price first and arrival time second. Each new order is matched at once against
the other side of the book and trades at the price of the resting order, so
one large order that sweeps several price levels can produce several trade
prices. Any unfilled part of a limit order stays in the book until it is
filled or a cancel record removes it (\Cref{sec:cancellations}). The
time-in-force field of an order is read from the data but not enforced, which
is a limitation of this implementation whose size is measured in
\Cref{sec:testfidelity}.

\subsection{The Batch Engine (FBA)}

The batch engine collects all orders arriving within an interval $\tau$ and
clears them at its end. It builds the total buy and total sell quantity at each
price, as in \eqref{eq:demandsupply}, using only submitted limit prices. A
market order counts as demand or supply at every price but never proposes a
price itself, so a batch made only of market orders does not trade. The engine
then picks the price with the largest traded volume. If several prices tie, it
applies four rules in turn: largest matched volume, then smallest leftover
imbalance, then the price closest to the previous batch's clearing price, and
finally the lowest price, so the outcome is always the same. Orders priced
strictly better than the clearing price are filled completely, except in the
rare case where a range of prices supports the same volume (\Cref{sec:testfba}).
Orders priced exactly at it are served in order of aggressiveness (a market order before any
limit order) and then submission time.

Volume that cannot be matched always moves to the next batch. No outside
source of liquidity, such as an automated market maker, is used to fill it, so
both engines can only trade against the orders that were actually submitted.
The interval is fixed at $\tau=1$\,second, which keeps a replay of about 640
million records computationally feasible while still being a meaningful
interval.

combination.

\subsection{How Cancellations and Other Records Are Treated}
\label{sec:cancellations}

Each record of the data is one status change of one order, and the replay turns
it into an action for the engines in a fixed way. A record that opens an order
(status \texttt{open}, or \texttt{triggered} for a conditional order) becomes a
new order. A record of one of the eight cancel types (cancelled by the trader,
scheduled or reduce-only cancel, cancel after a sibling order was filled,
self-trade cancel, margin cancel, vault-withdrawal cancel, liquidation cancel)
removes the order with the same order number from the engine: from the resting
book of the CDA, or from the waiting orders of the FBA, including any part that
was carried over from earlier batches. The whole order is removed, whatever size
it had left. If the order is no longer there, because it was already filled or
never seen, the cancellation does nothing and is not an error. Cancellations
are processed in file order, so a cancel that carries the same timestamp as a
new order is applied in the order the records appear. Rejections, and conditional
orders that have not triggered yet, never enter an engine.

The records that the venue wrote when \emph{its own} engine executed an order
(status \texttt{filled}) are deliberately not replayed. The two engines decide
fills themselves, and replaying the venue's fills as well would count them
twice. The consequence is that an order which the venue filled completely is
removed from a simulated book only if the simulation also fills it; otherwise it
stays until it is matched, or cancelled by a later record, which the venue never
sends for an order that is already filled. Cancellation records are therefore
the only way the simulated books shed orders other than by trading, and the size
of the books depends on them (\Cref{sec:testresets}).

\section{Data Source and Data Levels}
\label{sec:data}

The simulation uses the public Hyperliquid Level~4 order book dataset of
\citet{albers2026openbook}, recorded directly at a Hyperliquid node and
published on Zenodo: the full order-by-order feed, with participant
attribution, rejected orders, failed cancellations, both sides of every
trade and post-trade inventory, all at nanosecond resolution. Because it is
recorded at the node and not sampled from snapshots, the book does not have
to be reconstructed, which removes an important source of error.

Since the engines are the author's own, some quantities that are invisible in
real markets can be observed directly: every submitted price, including those
of orders that never trade, the exact rationing outcome, and the volume left
unmatched after each batch. Some things remain unknown. The dataset has no
oracle or mark price, so pricing error cannot be computed for either engine.
Orders that were never submitted visibly cannot be recovered. And timestamps
show when the node saw a message, not when the participant chose to send it.

\section{Experimental Design}
\label{sec:experiments}

The treatment is the matching engine, and the design tries to make everything
else identical, stating where it could not:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Same input.} Both runs read the same events in the same order:
  the full December~2025 SOL order-status archive, from its first record.
  \item \textbf{Same starting state.} Both engines start from an empty book at
  the same first timestamp, and no lead-in period is excluded from the
  metrics.
  \item \textbf{One uncontrolled case.} Neither engine prevents self-trades: an
  order that would fill against the same participant's own resting order is
  executed like any other. This is a real limitation, not a shared policy.
  \item \textbf{Determinism.} The same input always produces the same output,
  since nothing in either engine depends on randomness or on the clock. This
  holds for an uninterrupted replay; the month result was restarted several
  times in its first days (\Cref{sec:testresets}).
\end{enumerate}

The sample is a single instrument, SOL/USD, replayed once through each engine
over the whole month covered by the data: 1--31~December 2025, 744 hourly
files, none missing (the records themselves contain a few silences,
\Cref{sec:testfiles}). It is the full month, not a sample, so comparisons across
instruments or sessions are not possible. Each one-second interval provides one
CDA value and one FBA value from the same order flow, so differences are
measured as paired differences per interval. This removes variation caused by
the market itself and isolates the effect of the mechanism. Because market
data in consecutive seconds are correlated, the standard errors are
Newey--West errors, which allow for this correlation.

\section{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

The results report three groups of metrics, one for each part of RQ2: liquidity
and cost, price discovery, and execution and timing. The appendix defines every
metric with its exact formula.

\subsection{Interpretive Status of the RQ2.3 Metrics}
\label{sec:testable}

Order size inflation and boundary concentration only make sense as
\emph{reactions} to the mechanism, but the replayed orders were submitted on
Hyperliquid's own continuous engine, not in reaction to batching. Also, an
order's position relative to a simulated batch boundary depends on where the
boundary was placed, not on any choice by the participant. These two metrics
therefore show the mechanical effect of the rule that was implemented on fixed
historical orders. That is a useful baseline, but it is not evidence of how
participants would behave under real batching. Throughput and clearing latency
have a different purpose: they are a control, used to rule out differences in
processing speed as an explanation for anything else measured.

\section{Validation of the Harness}
\label{sec:validation}

A simulation is only as reliable as the code behind it. The order book checks a
small number of structural conditions all the time (for example that no price
level is left in the book with no orders), and if any fails the run stops
instead of writing a wrong row. Financial properties one would expect ---
quantities are conserved, no order trades beyond its limit, no fill is
counted twice --- are not checked live. They are checked beforehand by two
independent sets of tests: a suite of automatic tests, including comparisons
against a simple reference implementation, and a checklist of cases worked out
by hand, including the awkward ones (empty book, no crossing orders, every
tie-breaking step, a batch with one deciding order). The run finishing without
stopping is consistent with these properties holding throughout, but it is not
a new proof of them. Two checks against outside references were added: an
independent second implementation of both engines, and a comparison of the
simulated volume and price with what the venue itself recorded. Checking that
the FBA converges to the CDA as $\tau \to 0$ was not done, because it needs an
interval sweep that was not run. \Cref{app:testing} lists every test and every
data check, and its results are summarised in \Cref{sec:validationresults}.

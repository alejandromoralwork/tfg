\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Answers to the Research Questions}

This thesis asked two questions: how a frequent batch auction engine is
designed, and what changes when the same real order flow is cleared by
one instead of by a continuous book. The two paragraphs below answer each
in turn.

\paragraph{RQ1.} A frequent batch auction engine is fully specified by five
design blocks: the batch interval, the disclosure regime during
accumulation, the order flow segregation architecture, the clearing and
rationing rule, and settlement and residual handling. These are not
optional extras layered onto the mechanism but decisions that serial
processing settles automatically in a continuous engine, and which become
visible and contestable only once it is abandoned. \Cref{tab:consequences}
lists every option with its predicted consequence. Three points carry the
most weight. The only unambiguous structural gain is discrete accumulation
combined with uniform pricing; every other choice trades one distortion for
another, so the question is which distortion the venue prefers. The blocks
interact and cannot be optimised independently. And a venue can implement
frequent batch auctions exactly as proposed and still have a speed race,
through two separate channels: price-time rationing inside the batch
rewards being early, and a fixed, predictable close rewards being late ---
the sharpest practical implication of the analysis.
Two questions with no settled answer were developed as contributions: the
maker--taker distinction dissolves under simultaneous uniform-price
clearing, with persistence across batches the strongest of four candidate
redefinitions; and the price improvement uniform clearing produces is a
transfer between buyers and sellers, not a gain, whose split is set by the
clearing-price selection rule. Under a fixed close and a visible batch,
that split can be steered by whoever submits last, which turns the choice
of time window into a boundary repricing race (\Cref{sec:priceimprovement}).

\paragraph{RQ2.} The simulation replayed one month of SOL perpetual order
flow through both engines, with one-second batches. Its answer is that
batching changes \emph{who bears the cost of trading}, but not \emph{how
much} trades or \emph{at what price} (\Cref{tab:rq2answers}).

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Costs (RQ2.1).} In the continuous market, resting orders
  lose systematically to better-informed orders: the effective spread is
  positive, the realized spread negative and the price impact grows with
  time. In the batch market no such one-sided cost is found, because every
  order in a batch trades at one price set after all orders are in. Standing
  liquidity, however, is slightly lower in the batch market, not higher.
  \item \textbf{Prices (RQ2.2).} Both engines arrive at the same price, and
  volatility is about the same; uniform pricing removes the spread of prices
  within a second.
  \item \textbf{Execution (RQ2.3).} The same volume trades, but orders wait
  longer, because \FbaEmptyBatchShare\% of one-second batches do not cross at
  all and even those that do leave most of their longer side unmatched.
\end{itemize}

Put together, batching at one second protects resting orders from being
picked off, but on this market a one-second batch rarely holds enough
two-sided flow to work as the well-filled auction the mechanism assumes.
These conclusions hold for one instrument, one month and one interval, for
orders that were never submitted to a batch market, and the cost result
rests on an estimated trade direction, so it shows that no one-sided cost
is found rather than that the cost is zero (\Cref{sec:metricreliability}).

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The central limitation is intrinsic to the design. A replay holds order flow
constant, which is what makes the comparison clean, and by the same token
prevents participants from responding to the mechanism: the orders replayed
were submitted under a continuous engine, so their sizes were not chosen
against the rationing rule implemented (price-time-within-the-batch, a
different rule from the pro rata and size-priority alternatives surveyed
in \Cref{sec:rationing}), their arrival times
were not chosen against a batch boundary, and their limit prices were not
chosen under uncertainty about a clearing price. Every equilibrium
prediction of the FBA literature --- narrower quoted spreads, size
inflation, boundary concentration, bid shading --- is therefore out of
reach, and the simulation delivers the mechanical baseline against which
such responses would be measured. A further limitation bounds what future
work can build on: several metrics require adaptation to a batch setting,
which an extension inherits (\Cref{sec:metricreliability} works through them
case by case).

The sample itself bounds the claims further. The data come from one
venue, one instrument and one continuous month; a crypto perpetuals
exchange has a participant population, fee structure and latency profile
that differ from an equity market, so magnitudes should not be carried
over elsewhere without re-estimation, and the month gives day-by-day
stability within the sample, not a cross-instrument or cross-regime one.
And because the replay does not enforce post-only orders or self-trade
prevention, it trades about \VerCdaOverVenue\ times the venue's own recorded
volume (\Cref{sec:testfidelity}). Both engines receive this same input, so
it does not favour either side of the paired comparisons in
\Cref{ch:results}. Sizes are also rounded to whole SOL, which removes
orders below 0.5\,SOL from both engines alike (\Cref{sec:data}). But the
absolute volume, trade-count and latency levels reported there describe
this replay, not Hyperliquid's live market. Finally, the month was not
replayed in one uninterrupted run: \VerResetN\ restarts in its first five
days emptied both engines at the same record, which understates book-based
measures for some hours afterwards (\Cref{sec:testresets}).

\textbf{Scope of this chapter.} Every number and figure below comes from
one executed run: SOL/USD, the full month of 1--31~December 2025,

\Cref{app:config}. There is no interval sweep, no comparison of rationing
rules and no second instrument or period. Throughout,

\textbf{Inter-arrival time} is the time between one record and the next. Its
median (\SampleMedianInterArrival\,ms) is exactly zero because
\SampleZeroGapPct\% of consecutive records have the same nanosecond timestamp,
which suggests the node writes several events together, for example both fills
of one trade. The mean (\SampleMeanInterArrival\,ms) and the 99th percentile
(\SamplePNinetyNineInterArrival\,ms) show that between these bursts there are
real gaps of tens to hundreds of milliseconds. Records arrive in bursts, not at
a steady pace. This fits the empty-batch share: ``\FbaEmptyBatchShare\% of
batches empty'' means that in that second no buy price reached any sell price,
not that the second was quiet. Many messages and actual trades are different
things.

\textbf{Quoted spread} is the cost of crossing the book \emph{right now}. For
the CDA it is the gap between the best bid and the best ask; for the FBA it is
the gap between the best unfilled buy and the best unfilled sell left after a
batch clears. The two are close (\ResQuotedSpreadCda\,bps for the CDA versus
\ResQuotedSpreadFba\,bps for the FBA, a gap of \ResQuotedSpreadDiff\,bps) and
statistically different only because $n$ is over two million ($p$
\ResQuotedSpreadP). With a month of data even a small, steady gap is
significant, which does not make it economically important.

\textbf{Depth at best and depth within 10/50/100\,bps} both measure how much
can be traded, first at the best price and then cumulatively further away, and
only one of the two results can be trusted. Depth at best is higher for the FBA
(\ResDepthAtBestFba\ versus \ResDepthAtBestCda\,SOL), but this comes from how
it is defined, not from more liquidity. For the CDA it is the quantity on one
price level, since anything priced better would already have traded. For the FBA
it is $D(\clr)$ or $S(\clr)$ from \eqref{eq:demandsupply}, which adds up
\emph{all} orders priced at or better than the clearing price. So one price
level of a book is being compared with the whole aggressive side of a schedule
(\Cref{fig:depthcompare}).

Depth within a threshold avoids this problem. It counts quantity within a fixed
distance in price from a reference price (the CDA's midpoint, the FBA's own
$\clr$), and this measure is the same for both engines whatever the structure
of their books. On this fair measure the FBA is slightly \emph{thinner} than
the CDA at every threshold: \ResDepthWithinTenBpsFba\ against
\ResDepthWithinTenBpsCda\ within 10\,bps, \ResDepthWithinFiftyBpsFba\ against
\ResDepthWithinFiftyBpsCda\ within 50\,bps, and
\ResDepthWithinHundredBpsFba\ against \ResDepthWithinHundredBpsCda\ within
100\,bps (all $p$ \ResDepthWithinTenBpsP). If batching pooled liquidity we
would expect the opposite, and since this measure does not depend on
definitions, it is the more reliable of the two depth results.

\textbf{Effective spread, realized spread and price impact} split the cost of a
trade into parts. The effective spread is the total cost; the realized spread
is what the liquidity provider keeps after the price has moved; and the price
impact, the difference between the two, is the part lost to better-informed
traders. Theory predicts that if batching helps, the price impact should be
\emph{lower} under the FBA than under the CDA, because it is the part that
speed races inflate. All three need to know which side of
a trade was the aggressor, that is, who initiated it. In the CDA this is known
(the resting order is the maker, the crossing order the taker). In the FBA it
is not, because all orders of a batch trade together at one price and nobody
crosses anybody's book. The FBA side is therefore given an estimated direction
using the tick rule \citep{leeready1991}, based on the CDA's prices and not on
the FBA's own, which would be circular (\Cref{app:formulas} shows why). It is
applied over the \ResMatchedVolIntersectionN\ buckets in which both engines
traded: if the CDA price moved up, that second's FBA trades are labelled
buys; if it moved down, sells; if unchanged, the last label is kept
(\SignedFbaSpreadNSigned\ of \SignedFbaSpreadNMatched\ buckets can be labelled).
This is an estimate, not a real aggressor flag. The tick rule is usually 80--85\%
accurate against true trade direction, and using the \emph{other} engine's
prices adds further error, but it is the best option available.

With this estimate the FBA's spread is close to zero at every stage: effective
spread \ResEffectiveSpreadFba\,bps (CDA: \ResEffectiveSpreadCda\,bps),
realized spread
\ResRealizedSpreadOneSFba/\ResRealizedSpreadFiveSFba/\ResRealizedSpreadThirtySFba\,bps
at the three horizons (CDA:
\ResRealizedSpreadOneSCda/\ResRealizedSpreadFiveSCda/\ResRealizedSpreadThirtySCda\,bps),
and price impact
\ResPriceImpactOneSFba/\ResPriceImpactFiveSFba/\ResPriceImpactThirtySFba\,bps
(CDA:
\ResPriceImpactOneSCda/\ResPriceImpactFiveSCda/\ResPriceImpactThirtySCda\,bps),
all with $p<0.001$. The FBA values do not grow with the horizon, because in a
signed measure gains and losses cancel out over many trades, whereas a measure
of size only would keep growing with ordinary price movement. Price impact stays
positive at every horizon. Once the FBA side is given a plausible direction, it
shows no lasting cost to whichever side is labelled the taker. The CDA shows the
opposite: a positive effective spread, positive price impact and negative
realized spread, the typical pattern of a resting market maker losing to better
informed traders. This does not prove that FBA participants pay nothing; it
shows that the cost does not fall on one side, as it does in the CDA. It holds
on each of the 31 days and not only on average (\Cref{fig:dailytrend}).

Why the average ends up so close to zero is informative. Multiplying a fixed
size by an estimated sign gives an average near zero only when the sign is
almost unrelated to the true direction of the deviation. This agrees with the
Kyle's lambda result in \Cref{sec:res22}: the FBA's clearing price is tied to
its own \emph{previous} clearing price, so a single batch's order imbalance
barely moves it, while the CDA's price does react to imbalance. The borrowed
sign therefore says little about the FBA's true deviation, and the average
cancels out because the estimate cannot find the direction, not because the cost
is absent. The two near-zero results have one cause seen twice.

\textbf{Amihud illiquidity}, the price movement per unit of money traded, needs no
full order book. It is similar for both engines and slightly higher for the FBA
(\ResAmihudFba\ versus \ResAmihudCda, $p$ \ResAmihudP). This agrees with the
depth-within-threshold result: a small, consistent difference, not caused by a
definition, showing the FBA to be slightly less liquid at $\tau=1$\,second, not
more.

\textbf{Book imbalance and total book depth} do not exist for the FBA, which has
no resting book between clears, so they are described for the CDA only. The
volume left unmatched after a batch is not a good substitute, for two reasons.
A book can be read at any moment, whereas the FBA has either an unpriced
collection of orders or a finished batch, so there is no best price to compare
orders against until the batch is cleared and executed. And the unmatched volume
that passes to the next batch is not arranged into price levels; it is simply
considered again together with new orders, and in this month it is almost
entirely on one side (\Cref{sec:res23} gives a median unexecuted residual share
of essentially the whole longer side). It would show one lopsided queue, not the
two-sided structure that these metrics are meant to describe. The book
imbalance, $(V^{bid}-V^{ask})/(V^{bid}+V^{ask})$ at the best prices, averages
\DescBookImbalanceCdaMean\ (standard deviation \DescBookImbalanceCdaStd), close
to zero, as expected for a liquid two-sided book without lasting pressure on one
side. Total book depth averages \DescTotalBookDepthCdaMean\,SOL over the whole
book, the CDA's reference level for the depth comparison.

\textbf{Realized volatility}, measured inside each bucket, is not reported. It
needs two prices in the same bucket, and the FBA trades at most once per bucket,
so it almost never has them ($n=\ResRealizedVolNPaired$ of 2{,}678{,}400
buckets). \textbf{Matched-timestamp volatility} replaces it. It uses the buckets
in which \emph{both} engines traded: of the 2{,}678{,}400 buckets of the month,
\ResMatchedVolIntersectionN\ (\ResMatchedVolIntersectionPct\%) have a trade on
both sides. That is about four times the \ResMatchedVolIndependenceBaselinePct\%
that two \emph{unrelated} series, each with trades in roughly 23\% of buckets,
would share by chance, so both engines react to the same bursts of orders. In
this matched set, price changes between consecutive matched buckets are
computed at the same moments for both engines (\ResMatchedVolReturnsN\ price
changes), so each change covers exactly the same two instants on both sides,
however far apart they are. This common timeline keeps the comparison fair. The
changes are then summarised as a root-mean-square value per engine and calendar
day (in bps); each of the 31 days has more than the 5 changes required
(\ResMatchedVolWindowsExcluded\ days excluded). Daily volatility is similar for
both engines and slightly higher for the FBA (\ResMatchedVolFba\,bps versus
\ResMatchedVolCda\,bps, $p$ \ResMatchedVolP\ over $n=\ResMatchedVolNPaired$
days; \Cref{fig:matchedvol}). One caution applies. The measure only exists when
both engines trade in the same second, so it favours the busier periods. It is a
fair comparison between the engines on shared ground, not a volatility estimate
for the whole month of either engine.

\textbf{The intraday variant} keeps each price change as its own observation
instead of combining a day into one number: $n=\ResMatchedVolIntradayNPaired$,
each observation being the absolute price change in bps. It gives up the
averaging of the daily version in exchange for full time resolution on the same
common timeline. The result points the same way and is more precise: CDA
\ResMatchedVolIntradayCda\,bps versus FBA \ResMatchedVolIntradayFba\,bps ($p$
\ResMatchedVolIntradayP), a difference of \ResMatchedVolIntradayDiff\,bps
compared with \ResMatchedVolDiff\,bps for the daily version. The daily value is
the more robust measure of a typical day; the intraday one shows how volatility
is spread over each second.

\textbf{Intra-interval price dispersion} measures how far apart the trade prices
are \emph{within} one interval. For the FBA it is about zero, as designed,
because every trade in a batch is at the same price $\clr$. The CDA value,
\ResDispersionCda\,bps, shows how much price variation within an interval
uniform pricing removes.

\textbf{Kyle's lambda} measures how much the price moves for each unit of net
order imbalance, the standard measure of the price impact of order flow. It is
built differently for the two engines, so the difference in the table should be
read with care and the two separate values are the useful comparison.
\Cref{fig:kylelambda} shows the full distribution: the FBA value stays tightly
around zero all month, while the CDA value is wider and skewed to positive
values. This fits the FBA's rule of anchoring to the previous clearing price,
which makes its price hardly react to the imbalance of a single batch, a
protection that a continuous book does not have.

\Cref{fig:pricediff} shows how closely the two lines follow each other. The
difference between the FBA and CDA VWAP, over \PriceDiffN\ intervals in which
both engines traded, has a median of \PriceDiffMedianBps\,bps and a mean of
\PriceDiffMeanBps\,bps, which is practically zero, with a standard deviation of
\PriceDiffStdBps\,bps. The usual gap is under a basis point and only reaches
tens of bps in the sharp move around 14:00--15:00 visible in
\Cref{fig:pricepaths}. Two mechanisms trading the same orders do not drift apart;
they differ only when the market moves fast. The link is direct: the correlation
between $|\text{FBA}-\text{CDA}|$ and CDA realized volatility is
\PriceDiffVolCorr\ over the month, well above its correlation with either
engine's trade count (under 0.25), and the largest differences occur with
\emph{more} trades on both sides than average, so they reflect volatility and
not thin data. The reason is mechanical. The CDA's VWAP averages every fill as
the book is swept within the second, while the FBA clears once at one price, so
in a fast market the two ways of defining ``the price of this second'' can
differ by tens of bps without either being wrong. A few isolated seconds have a
simpler cause: one engine trades almost nothing while the other clears a backlog.

\textbf{Executed volume and notional} (\ResExecVolumeCda\ versus
\ResExecVolumeFba\,SOL per interval, \ResExecNotionalCda\ versus
\ResExecNotionalFba\,USD per interval) are not statistically different ($p$
\ResExecVolumeP\ and $p$ \ResExecNotionalP). These are means over the
2,678,400 one-second intervals of the month, not month totals; summed over
the whole month they are the \$\SampleCdaNotional\ and \$\SampleFbaNotional\
of \Cref{tab:sample}. This is the clearest confirmation that the comparison
is fair: both engines can only trade against the orders that were actually
submitted, so this row checks the replay more than it describes the
mechanism.

\textbf{Trade count} is slightly higher for the FBA (\ResTradeCountFba\ versus
\ResTradeCountCda\ trades per interval, $p$ \ResTradeCountP). With almost the
same total volume this means slightly smaller trades, consistent with one
clearing price sometimes splitting a large order among several counterparties.

\textbf{Fill rate and time to execution} together show the cost of waiting in a
batch. Fill rate is measured in the second in which the order was submitted, so
at $\tau=1$\,second, much shorter than most orders' time to fill, a low value is
expected and does not mean few orders are eventually filled. Time to execution
is the complementary number. Only a mean per bucket is recorded, so no median or
95th percentile across individual orders is available.

\textbf{Trader surplus} measures the price improvement relative to the
submitted limit price that uniform pricing gives. Theory says it should
be positive for every FBA order that is not marginal; the useful comparison is
the difference from the CDA, where trades happen at the resting order's price.

\textbf{Order size inflation}, the ratio of submitted to filled size per
participant, is the typical sign of \emph{pro rata} rationing: participants who
know their share grows with the size they submit have a reason to submit more
than they want. It is \emph{lower} on the FBA side (\ResSizeInflationFba\ versus
\ResSizeInflationCda, $p$ \ResSizeInflationP), which might look like a benefit
of batching. But the rule implemented here is price-time within the batch, not
pro rata, so this flow has no such incentive to react to. The result is the
mechanical effect of one rule on fixed historical orders, not evidence that
batching reduces size inflation.

\textbf{Order-to-trade ratio}, messages sent per executed trade, is lower for the
FBA (\ResOrderToTradeRatioFba\ versus \ResOrderToTradeRatioCda, $p$
\ResOrderToTradeRatioP). With nearly the same trade count, this means fewer
messages per trade. That is plausible because resting orders in the CDA are
exposed to being picked off from tick to tick and may be cancelled defensively,
which the FBA's single clearing per second dampens.

\textbf{Boundary concentration} (FBA only) is the share of orders arriving in the
last 10\% of a batch window, which would show participants timing their orders
against a predictable closing time. Its mean, \DescBoundaryConcentrationFbaMean\
(10.1\%), is almost exactly the 10\% that evenly spread arrivals would give, and
the median is lower (\DescBoundaryConcentrationFbaMedian, 6.6\%). There is no
clustering beyond chance, as expected, since these orders had no real batch
boundary to time against. It is the baseline against which real timing behaviour
would be measured, not evidence that such behaviour would not appear in real
batching.

\textbf{Unexecuted residual share} is
$\Sigma|\mathrm{demand}(\clr)-\mathrm{supply}(\clr)| /
\Sigma\max(\mathrm{demand}(\clr),\mathrm{supply}(\clr))$. It shows how unbalanced
the two sides are at the clearing price; it is \emph{not} the share of submitted
volume left unfilled. A batch can trade everything it possibly can and still have
a high value if one side was much larger than the other. The median over the
month is exactly $1.00$, so a fully one-sided batch is the typical case, not the
exception. \Cref{fig:residualshare} shows this is stable from day to day: the 31
daily means lie between $0.93$ and $0.99$, a structural feature of one-second
batches on this instrument and not a few extreme episodes.

\textbf{Engine controls} test whether the implementation could explain the
results, not describe the mechanisms themselves. The mean latency gap looks
extreme (\ResClearingLatencyCda\,$\mu$s for the CDA against
\ResClearingLatencyFba\,$\mu$s for the FBA, three orders of magnitude) because
the two numbers time different amounts of work: the CDA's figure is the cost of
matching one order against the book, while the FBA's is the cost of clearing one
\emph{whole batch} --- building the demand and supply schedule from every order
in it and rationing every eligible one, all in a single timed operation. New live
orders arrive at roughly a hundred per second on average across the month, so an
average non-empty batch (\FbaEmptyBatchShare\% of batches hold none at all) is
clearing that many orders' worth of work in one measurement; a gap of that rough
order of magnitude between one order and one batch is therefore expected from
what is being measured, not evidence that the FBA algorithm itself is that much
slower. Throughput
compares the two more fairly, since its numerator is the same message count for
both engines: there the gap narrows to \ResThroughputCda\ against
\ResThroughputFba\ orders per second, under four times, which is closer to a
genuine per-message cost --- clearing a batch means searching over every
candidate price and sorting every eligible order, more work per order handled
than the CDA's lookup against a couple of book levels. Either way, both figures
stay far inside what real-time trading needs, and the CDA is faster on both
measures, which rules out the reverse concern: the FBA's results are not an
artifact of the CDA being slowed by heavy load (\Cref{fig:latencycontrol}).

\section{Summary of Findings}
\label{sec:res_summary}

This section steps back from the individual metrics and asks what they add up
to. Read together rather than table by table, the numbers above support five
conclusions.

\textbf{1. Four different metrics all say the same thing: in the FBA, nobody
pays the cost that a resting order pays in the CDA.} Effective spread,
realized spread, price impact and Kyle's lambda measure this cost from four
different angles --- quoted prices, prices some seconds after the trade, the
gap between the two, and how far a batch's own price moves for a given order
imbalance. The first three are built the same way: each needs a trade's
direction, estimated for the FBA from the CDA's own tick-by-tick price moves
(\Cref{sec:res21}). Kyle's lambda is not built that way at all --- it only
needs each batch's own submitted buy and sell quantities, which are simply
known, with no direction to estimate. Three of the four share one
construction choice; the fourth does not, and it tells the same story
anyway.

In the CDA, the pattern is the textbook one for adverse selection. The
effective spread is positive (\ResEffectiveSpreadCda\,bps), and the maker
does not keep it: the realized spread is \emph{negative} at all three
horizons tested
(\ResRealizedSpreadOneSCda/\ResRealizedSpreadFiveSCda/\ResRealizedSpreadThirtySCda\,bps
at 1/5/30\,s). So the price impact --- the part of the cost lost to
better-informed traders --- is positive, and it grows the longer you wait
after the trade
(\ResPriceImpactOneSCda\,$\to$\,\ResPriceImpactFiveSCda\,$\to$\,\ResPriceImpactThirtySCda\,bps),
which is what happens as the market slowly absorbs what that trade revealed.
Kyle's lambda tells the same story a fourth way: at \ResKyleLambdaCda\,bps
per SOL of net flow, the CDA's price visibly moves when one side of the
market dominates.

Every one of those four numbers is close to zero for the FBA instead.
Effective spread: \ResEffectiveSpreadFba\,bps. Realized spread: within
\ResRealizedSpreadOneSFba\,--\,\ResRealizedSpreadThirtySFba\,bps of zero, at
every horizon. Price impact: still under \ResPriceImpactThirtySFba\,bps even
30 seconds after the trade. Kyle's lambda: \ResKyleLambdaFba\,bps per SOL, an
order of magnitude below the CDA's. Every one of these differences is
significant at $p<0.001$, on samples of half a million or more paired
intervals, and \Cref{fig:dailytrend} shows this is not one lucky month-long
average: the FBA's effective spread stays near zero on every one of the 31
days taken separately.

One caveat is worth repeating here. The FBA has no real record of who was
buyer and who was seller in a batch, so effective spread, realized spread
and price impact all had to be estimated with a tick rule
(\Cref{sec:res21}) --- the best available estimate, not a certainty, and
since all three share it, their agreement with each other is not three
independent confirmations on its own. Kyle's lambda breaks that
dependence: built with no estimated direction at all, its agreement with
the other three is a genuinely separate check, not a repeat of the same
one in different clothes. It is also exactly what the theory in
Chapter~\ref{ch:foundations} predicts:
clearing everyone at one uniform price removes the reason to race a resting
order, because in a batch there is no resting order left to race.

\textbf{2. This is not because the FBA trades less, trades different orders,
or trades at a different price.} Three checks confirm this. Executed volume
and notional are statistically indistinguishable between the two engines
($p=\ResExecVolumeP$/$\ResExecNotionalP$). Trade count is almost identical
(\ResTradeCountCda\ against \ResTradeCountFba\ trades per interval). And the
two engines' own price series, which nothing forces to agree, track each
other closely: a median difference of \PriceDiffMedianBps\,bps and a mean of
\PriceDiffMeanBps\,bps, widening only during the fastest price moves of the
month (the size of the gap correlates \PriceDiffVolCorr\ with realized
volatility, far more than with how many orders arrived).

So the near-zero cost in point~1 is not a side effect of a thinner or slower
market quietly doing less business --- both engines are clearing essentially
the same flow at essentially the same price. What changes between them is
not \emph{how much} trades, or \emph{at what price}. It is \emph{who pays for
it}. In the CDA, the resting side pays every time a better-informed order
arrives against it. In the FBA, nobody in particular pays, because everyone
in the batch trades together at the one price the batch produces.

\textbf{3. The cost that disappears from the spread turns up somewhere else:
in depth, in delay, and in how often a batch has two sides at all.} Three
separate signs point the same way.

First, depth within a fixed distance of the reference price --- the one
depth measure that does not depend on how each engine happens to define
``at the best,'' and so the one that is actually comparable --- is slightly
\emph{lower} for the FBA at every threshold tested (10/50/100\,bps: FBA
\ResDepthWithinTenBpsFba/\ResDepthWithinFiftyBpsFba/\ResDepthWithinHundredBpsFba\
against CDA
\ResDepthWithinTenBpsCda/\ResDepthWithinFiftyBpsCda/\ResDepthWithinHundredBpsCda,
all $p<0.001$). If batching pooled many participants' \emph{standing}
liquidity, not just their timing, this should have come out the other way.
Amihud illiquidity agrees: a given amount of trading moves the FBA's price
marginally more, not less (\ResAmihudFba\ against \ResAmihudCda,
$p<0.001$).

Second, a one-second batch on this market is rarely the well-filled, two-sided
auction the mechanism is meant to produce. \FbaEmptyBatchShare\% of batches
clear no trade at all. In the batches that do trade, the larger side is
typically left almost entirely unmatched: the unexecuted residual share
averages \DescResidualShareFbaMean\ and its median is exactly
\DescResidualShareFbaMedian, so a fully one-sided batch is the ordinary
outcome, not a rare one. \Cref{fig:residualshare} shows this every single
day of the month (daily means between 0.93 and 0.99) --- it is a structural
feature of this instrument at this interval, not a handful of bad episodes.

Third, execution simply takes longer under the FBA: \ResTimeToExecFba\,s on
average, against \ResTimeToExecCda\,s for the CDA, a gap of
\ResTimeToExecDiff\,s. This is the one place in the whole chapter where the
FBA is unambiguously worse, not just differently measured.

None of this means the mechanism failed. It means that at $\tau=1$\,second,
on a market where most one-second windows see no crossing trade at all, a
batch usually does not have enough order flow on both sides for the
coordination the mechanism relies on to do its work. A longer $\tau$ would
change how full a batch is; whether it would do so without giving back
point~1's gain is a question this thesis's single-interval design cannot
answer.

\textbf{4. A few results are simply the rule working as written, and mostly
confirm that the replay is correct rather than teach something new about
batching.} Trader surplus is marginally higher under the FBA
(\ResTraderSurplusFba\ against \ResTraderSurplusCda\,USD per interval) ---
exactly what uniform pricing should do, since it gives every non-marginal
order a better price than it asked for. Order size inflation is lower under
the FBA (\ResSizeInflationFba\ against \ResSizeInflationCda), but the
rationing rule actually implemented here is price-time, not pro rata
(\Cref{sec:rationing}), and price-time gives nobody a reason to inflate their
order size in the first place. A lower number here shows the rule doing
exactly what it is supposed to do, not that batching itself discourages
stuffing. Boundary concentration sits almost exactly at the 10\% that random,
untimed arrivals would give by chance (\DescBoundaryConcentrationFbaMean, or
10.1\%; median \DescBoundaryConcentrationFbaMedian, or 6.6\%) --- unsurprising, since these
historical orders had no real batch boundary to time against in the first
place. And the three-orders-of-magnitude gap in raw clearing latency is an
artefact of timing one order against timing one whole batch, not a real
per-order slowdown (\Cref{sec:res23}); throughput, the fairer comparison,
still leaves the FBA comfortably inside what real-time trading needs. All
four results are useful precisely because they turned out as expected: they
are evidence the simulation is doing what it claims to, not new findings
about the mechanism.

\textbf{5. All four conclusions above hold for exactly this replay, and no
further.} Every number in this chapter is the mechanical output of one fixed
rule applied to orders that were actually submitted for Hyperliquid's own
continuous engine, not in reaction to being cleared in batches.
\Cref{sec:testable} explains why the behavioural predictions this thesis
could not test --- market makers requoting, bids shaded under price
uncertainty, size inflation under a rule that would actually reward it,
orders bunching before a real batch boundary --- all need participants who
can respond to the mechanism, and a replay of fixed history cannot supply
that.

Two further limits are worth stating plainly. The conclusions above hold for
one instrument (SOL/USD), one month (December 2025), one interval
($\tau=1$\,s) and one rationing rule (price-time); none of this was rerun at
a different interval or rule, so whether a longer $\tau$ would fill batches
without losing point~1's near-zero cost is a question this thesis raises and
does not answer. And \Cref{app:testing} shows the replay itself trades
roughly twice the volume the real venue recorded, because time-in-force is
read but not enforced. Both engines share this defect identically, so it
does not favour either side of the comparisons above --- but it does mean
the \emph{absolute} volume, trade-count and latency levels in this chapter
describe this replay, not Hyperliquid's live market.

Put together: on this real order flow, clearing it in one-second batches
instead of continuously removes the adverse-selection cost a resting maker
pays, without changing how much trades or at what price. It pays for that
with thinner depth, slower execution, and a market that, at this interval,
usually does not have two sides at once. That is a mechanical description of
what one specific rule does to one month of real orders that were never
asked to react to it --- not a general verdict on frequent batch auctions,
and not evidence of how real participants, free to requote, resize or retime
their orders, would behave under one.

\section{Reliability and Interpretation of the Metrics}
\label{sec:metricreliability}

The conclusions above are only as good as the metrics behind them, and not
every metric in this chapter earns the same trust. Two engines with
different internal structures --- one a continuous book, one a periodic
auction --- do not always have a natural common yardstick, and where one
had to be built rather than simply read off, that construction can leave a
bias of its own. This section goes back through every metric family used
above, states plainly what it measures, how far the comparison can be
trusted, what could bias it, and what conclusion survives that bias
regardless. The short version: metrics built directly from executed trades
are the most trustworthy; metrics that had to borrow a missing piece of
information from the other engine are the least; and the headline findings
of \Cref{sec:res_summary} were deliberately built from the first group,
corroborated from several independent angles, precisely because of this.

\subsection{Highest confidence: metrics read directly off executed trades}

Executed volume, executed notional, trade count and VWAP (\Cref{sec:res23})
need no construction choice at all: a trade either happened or it did not,
at a price and a quantity that are facts of the replay, identical in kind
for both engines. There is no proxy, no borrowed signal, nothing to
calibrate. These are the metrics behind point~2 of
\Cref{sec:res_summary} (the two engines trade statistically the same flow
at the same price), and that conclusion is about as close to unconditionally
reliable as anything in this thesis: it would take an error in the
replay itself, not a definitional mismatch between the engines, to
overturn it.

\subsection{The tick-rule-signed cost metrics}

Effective spread, realized spread and price impact (\Cref{sec:res21}) are
the headline result of this chapter, and also the metrics that lean
hardest on a construction choice. The CDA knows which side of every trade
was the aggressor; the FBA does not, since a batch has no resting order
to be crossed. The FBA's side of all three numbers is therefore signed
with a tick rule read off the \emph{CDA's} price series (\Cref{sec:res21}
explains why the FBA's own price history cannot be used without
circularity). Two biases follow directly from this. First, the tick rule
itself is an estimate: it is usually 80--85\% accurate against true trade
direction even with full trade-level data, so every FBA-side number in
\Cref{tab:res21} should be read as \emph{directionally} informative, not
as a precise point estimate of a real aggressor-flagged quantity. Second,
and more consequentially, all three metrics share this one construction:
the same borrowed sign, applied to the same trades. Their agreement with
each other is therefore not three independent confirmations --- a
systematic bias in that one shared choice would move all three together,
not cancel out.

What breaks that dependence is Kyle's lambda (\Cref{sec:res22}). Unlike
the other three, it needs no borrowed sign at all: it regresses each
batch's own price change on its net submitted order flow, buy quantity
minus sell quantity, a quantity read directly off the orders themselves
with nothing to estimate. Kyle's lambda is therefore the one cost metric
in this chapter genuinely free of the tick-rule construction, and it
tells the same story as the other three: an order of magnitude smaller
for the FBA than the CDA. That agreement has a real economic explanation,
not a statistical coincidence: the FBA's clearing price is anchored to
its own previous clearing price, so a single batch's own order imbalance
barely moves it, regardless of how any trade inside that batch later gets
signed. The conclusion this chapter is willing to stand behind follows
from that structure: Kyle's lambda, built without the tick rule,
independently confirms that the FBA's price does not move much for a
given order imbalance, for a real and understood reason, which gives the
tick-rule trio's shared near-zero result somewhere to stand. The
\emph{sign and rough size} of the effect is what survives this scrutiny;
the precise FBA point estimates for effective spread, realized spread and
price impact should still not be read to the decimal, since all three
remain hostage to the one construction choice they share.

\subsection{Depth and quoted-spread analogues}

Three liquidity metrics look alike but are not equally trustworthy.
\textbf{Depth at best} compares a single CDA book level against the FBA's
entire eligible side at the clearing price (\Cref{sec:res21}) ---
structurally different objects, so the result that the FBA looks
``deeper'' here is a definitional artefact, not evidence of more standing
liquidity, and should not be used on its own. \textbf{Depth within a fixed
distance of the reference price} uses the same definition on both sides
(quantity within $x$ basis points of the CDA midpoint or the FBA clearing
price) and is the one of the three that is actually comparable; its
result --- the FBA is slightly \emph{thinner}, not deeper, at every
threshold --- is the one this thesis relies on. \textbf{Quoted spread} sits
between the two: the FBA side is read from the post-clearing residual,
and only from batches where that residual is two-sided --- the rare
case rather than the typical one for this instrument at $\tau=1$\,s, as
\Cref{app:comparability} explains in full, along with why the FBA's
unmatched order set is not, in general, usable as a stand-in for a CDA
order book at all. The quoted-spread numbers in \Cref{tab:res21} are
therefore computed correctly, but only over that selected, two-sided
minority of batches: they describe how tight the book looks on the rare
batches that resemble an ordinary two-sided market, not a month-wide
average the way the CDA's quoted spread does. Read that narrowly, the
near-equal CDA/FBA gap reported here is trustworthy; read as a general
claim about FBA liquidity, it is not --- which is exactly why depth
within a threshold, not quoted spread, carries the liquidity conclusion
in \Cref{sec:res_summary}.

\subsection{Engine controls: throughput and clearing latency}

\Cref{sec:res23}'s engine-control rows exist to rule out one specific
confound --- that a measured difference reflects which engine the
computer happens to run faster, not what the mechanism does --- and that
is also exactly where their own bias lives. Mean clearing latency times
one CDA order against one whole FBA batch, so the three-orders-of-magnitude
gap it shows is an artefact of what is being timed, not a real per-order
slowdown. Throughput, whose numerator is the same message count for both
engines, is the fairer of the two and still shows the CDA finishing faster
per unit of work; because both figures are measured on the same machine
under the same load, the direction of that gap is trustworthy even though
neither number is a claim about market quality. Both are also wall-clock
measurements and so are the only two of the 35 output columns that are not
bit-for-bit deterministic across repeated runs (\Cref{sec:testdeterminism})
--- a property of how they are measured, not evidence against the engines
themselves.

\subsection{Mechanically true, not behaviourally tested}

Order size inflation and boundary concentration are computed correctly,
but what they can support is narrower than it looks. Both describe
properties of orders that were submitted to Hyperliquid's own continuous
engine, not in reaction to being cleared in batches, so a result like ``no
clustering beyond chance'' is a fact about this fixed order flow under the
rule implemented, not a prediction about what real participants would do
if they actually faced a predictable batch boundary or a pro rata
incentive to inflate size (\Cref{sec:testable} sets out this distinction
in full). The conclusion these metrics support is deliberately modest:
they show the mechanical rule behaving as specified on real data, which is
evidence the simulation is correct, not evidence about equilibrium
behaviour under batching.

\subsection{What the replay itself gets right or wrong}

Two further checks bound every number in this chapter from underneath.
Internally, a measured CDA/FBA difference could in principle reflect a
difference in how well each engine happens to be implemented rather than
the mechanism itself. This is mitigated by the two engines sharing one
order and trade representation and one metrics implementation,
reinforced by the independent reference-implementation check and the
differential test suite (\Cref{sec:validation,app:testing}); the single
strongest such check, convergence of the FBA to the CDA as $\tau\to0$,
was not run. Externally, the replay does not enforce time-in-force, so it
trades roughly twice the real venue's recorded volume. Both engines share
this gap identically, so it cancels out of every paired comparison in
this chapter, but it does mean the \emph{absolute} volume, trade-count
and latency levels describe this replay, not Hyperliquid's live market.

\subsection{Reading This Chapter}

Ranked by how much a reader should lean on them: the trade-based metrics
(volume, notional, trade count, VWAP, depth within a threshold) are safe
to take near-literally; the tick-rule-signed cost metrics are safe to read
for sign and rough magnitude, since the one of the four built without
that construction, Kyle's lambda, independently tells the same story; the
engine-control and mechanical-only metrics are safe
only for the narrow claims --- no compute confound, no behavioural
response --- they were built to support; and depth at best, quoted
spread, and any reading of the FBA's unmatched orders as ``the FBA order
book'' should not be trusted beyond what \Cref{app:comparability}
specifically licenses. None of this weakens the headline result: it is
precisely because the four independent, trade-grounded cost metrics agree
with each other, and because the thinner-not-deeper depth result survives
on the one definition that is genuinely comparable, that
\Cref{sec:res_summary}'s conclusions rest on the most reliable tier of
evidence this chapter has to offer.

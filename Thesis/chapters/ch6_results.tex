\chapter{Results: Comparing CDA and FBA}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter reports the results of one executed run: the SOL perpetual, the
full month of 1--31~December 2025, $\tau = 1$\,second, and the single fixed
engine configuration of \Cref{app:config}. Throughout, a \emph{bucket} is one
of the 2,678,400 consecutive one-second windows of the month, and every metric
is computed per bucket. Each paired difference (FBA$-$CDA) uses only the
buckets in which the metric exists for both engines (the $n$ column) and a
Newey--West standard error (\Cref{sec:experiments}). Sections
\ref{sec:res21}--\ref{sec:res23} report the results, \Cref{sec:res_summary}
summarises them, and \Cref{sec:metricreliability} sets out the limits on how
far they can be read.

\paragraph{Main findings.} The comparison yields six findings, each
developed in the section given.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{F\arabic*}]
  \item \textbf{Batching leaves the volume and the price unchanged.} Both
  engines trade the same volume (\ResExecVolumeCda\ against
  \ResExecVolumeFba\,SOL per second, $p$ \ResExecVolumeP), and the median
  gap between their prices is \PriceDiffMedianBps\,bps
  (\Cref{sec:res22,sec:res23}).
  \item \textbf{The price responds to order flow by the same amount.}
  Five seconds after the venue's net taker flow, both prices have moved by
  the same amount per unit of flow (Kyle's lambda
  \ResCommonFlowLambdaFiveSCda\ against \ResCommonFlowLambdaFiveSFba\,bps
  per 1{,}000 SOL, $p$ \ResCommonFlowLambdaFiveSP; \Cref{sec:res22}).
  \item \textbf{Uniform pricing removes price dispersion within a second.}
  Dispersion falls from \ResDispersionCda\ to \ResDispersionFba\,bps, and
  is exactly zero in \DispersionFbaExactZeroPct\% of the seconds in which
  both engines traded (\Cref{sec:res22}).
  \item \textbf{At one second, batching adds no liquidity.} Depth within
  10, 50 and 100\,bps of the price is slightly lower for the FBA, not
  higher; Amihud illiquidity and volatility are also slightly higher, but
  part of that gap comes from how each engine's price is measured
  (\Cref{sec:res21,sec:res22}).
  \item \textbf{Batching delays execution.} Orders wait
  \ResTimeToExecDiff\,s longer for their first fill. Most one-second
  batches (\FbaEmptyBatchShare\%) clear no trade, about the same share of
  seconds in which the CDA trades nothing, so a batch rarely holds more
  than the flow that would have traded anyway (\Cref{sec:res23}).
  \item \textbf{The continuous market charges resting orders an
  adverse-selection cost.} In the CDA the realized spread is negative and
  the price impact grows from \ResPriceImpactOneSCda\ to
  \ResPriceImpactThirtySCda\,bps between 1 and 30\,s (\Cref{sec:res21}).
  Whether batching removes this cost cannot be measured in the replay
  (\Cref{sec:limitssign}).
\end{enumerate}
\Cref{sec:findingsreliability} explains how each finding is reached and
how far it can be relied on, and why each result arises; F1, F2, F3, F5
and F6 are well supported, while F4 supports only the weaker statement
that batching does not make the market more liquid.

\textbf{Inter-arrival time} is the time between one record and the next. Its
median (\SampleMedianInterArrival\,ms) is exactly zero because
\SampleZeroGapPct\% of consecutive records have the same nanosecond timestamp,
as expected on a venue that processes orders block by block
(\Cref{sec:venue}). The mean (\SampleMeanInterArrival\,ms) and the 99th
percentile (\SamplePNinetyNineInterArrival\,ms) show the gaps between these
bursts, of tens to hundreds of milliseconds. This also explains the
empty-batch share: ``\FbaEmptyBatchShare\% of batches empty'' means that in
that second no buy limit reached any sell limit (a batch of only market
orders does not trade either, \Cref{sec:environment}), not that the second
was quiet. The continuous market is no different in this respect: the CDA
also trades in only about a quarter of all seconds (\Cref{sec:res22}),
because in most seconds no order arrives that is priced to cross the book.

\textbf{Quoted spread.} The two engines are close: \ResQuotedSpreadCda\,bps
for the CDA and \ResQuotedSpreadFba\,bps for the FBA, a gap of
\ResQuotedSpreadDiff\,bps. The difference is statistically significant ($p$
\ResQuotedSpreadP) only because $n$ is over two million; it is too small to
matter economically.

\textbf{Depth: the FBA is slightly thinner (F4).} Depth within a fixed
distance of the reference price is defined the same way for both engines,
and on it the FBA is slightly \emph{thinner} at every threshold:
\ResDepthWithinTenBpsFba\ against \ResDepthWithinTenBpsCda\,SOL within
10\,bps, \ResDepthWithinFiftyBpsFba\ against \ResDepthWithinFiftyBpsCda\
within 50\,bps, and \ResDepthWithinHundredBpsFba\ against
\ResDepthWithinHundredBpsCda\ within 100\,bps (all $p$
\ResDepthWithinTenBpsP; \Cref{fig:depthcompare}). If batching pooled
liquidity, the FBA would be deeper. Depth at best is higher for the FBA
(\ResDepthAtBestFba\ against \ResDepthAtBestCda\,SOL), but the two
figures measure different objects and are not compared
(\Cref{sec:limitscomparable}).

\textbf{Spread decomposition: resting orders lose in the CDA (F6).}
Effective spread, realized spread and price impact split the cost of a
trade into the total cost, the part the liquidity provider keeps once the
price has moved, and the part lost to better-informed traders. The CDA
shows the textbook pattern of adverse selection: a positive
effective spread (\ResEffectiveSpreadCda\,bps), a \emph{negative} realized
spread at every horizon
(\ResRealizedSpreadOneSCda/\ResRealizedSpreadFiveSCda/\ResRealizedSpreadThirtySCda\,bps
at 1/5/30\,s), and a positive price impact that grows with the horizon
(\ResPriceImpactOneSCda/\ResPriceImpactFiveSCda/\ResPriceImpactThirtySCda\,bps).
The realized spread is the revenue of the \emph{resting} side of a trade,
the maker, which in a continuous market is mostly a market maker; the
taker's cost is the effective spread. A negative realized spread means
that the maker earned the effective spread at the moment of the trade
but lost more than that once the price moved: the price impact exceeds
the effective spread. On average a resting order therefore loses once the
price has absorbed what the trade revealed, and only the makers bear this
loss. The FBA's values (\Cref{fig:spreaddecomp,fig:dailytrend}) are
close to zero by construction and are reported but not interpreted
(\Cref{sec:limitssign}); the comparison between the engines rests on the
measures that need no trade direction, Amihud illiquidity below and
Kyle's lambda in \Cref{sec:res22}.

\textbf{Amihud illiquidity: higher for the FBA (F4).} The price movement
per dollar traded is about a fifth higher for the FBA (\ResAmihudFba\
against \ResAmihudCda, $p$ \ResAmihudP). Part of this gap is a matter of
measurement. The CDA's reference price is the book midpoint, which does
not move when trades alternate between the bid and the ask; the FBA's is
the clearing price, which lands on the ask side when buyers dominate a
batch and on the bid side when sellers do, so it moves by about the
spread between such batches even when the midpoint does not. The Amihud
gap therefore overstates how much less liquid the FBA is; together with
the depth result it shows that the FBA is not more liquid.

\textbf{Volatility: slightly higher for the FBA (F4).} Volatility within a
bucket cannot be compared, because the FBA almost never has two prices in
one bucket ($n=\ResRealizedVolNPaired$); matched-timestamp volatility
replaces it. Both
engines traded in \ResMatchedVolIntersectionN\ buckets
(\ResMatchedVolIntersectionPct\%), about four times the
\ResMatchedVolIndependenceBaselinePct\% that two unrelated series with
trades in roughly 23\% of buckets would share by chance, so both engines
react to the same bursts of orders. Price changes between consecutive
matched buckets (\ResMatchedVolReturnsN\ changes) cover exactly the same
instants for both engines, and their daily root mean square is similar for
both, slightly higher for the FBA (\ResMatchedVolFba\ against
\ResMatchedVolCda\,bps, $p$ \ResMatchedVolP\ over $n=\ResMatchedVolNPaired$
days, \ResMatchedVolWindowsExcluded\ days excluded; \Cref{fig:matchedvol}).
No dampening of price moves (\Cref{tab:theorylinks}) is found at
$\tau = 1$\,s. The small excess for the FBA is consistent with how the two
prices are formed: the CDA's VWAP averages several fills within the
second, which smooths it, while the FBA's single clearing price does not.
The measure covers the busier seconds in which both engines trade; it is a
comparison on common ground, not a volatility estimate for the whole month.

\textbf{Intra-interval price dispersion: removed by uniform pricing (F3).}
The spread of trade prices within one second is \ResDispersionCda\,bps for
the CDA and practically
zero for the FBA, as uniform pricing implies: exactly zero in
\DispersionFbaExactZeroPct\% of the intervals in which both engines traded,
the rest being seconds that received trades from two batch clears
(\Cref{sec:testresets}).

\textbf{Kyle's lambda: the same response to order flow (F2).} Both
engines' prices follow the venue's net taker flow
(\Cref{fig:kylelambda}, left): net buying raises the price and net
selling lowers it, as adverse selection predicts. Measured 5\,s after the
flow, the two engines respond by the same amount,
\ResCommonFlowLambdaFiveSCda\ against \ResCommonFlowLambdaFiveSFba\,bps per
1{,}000 SOL ($p$ \ResCommonFlowLambdaFiveSP). Batching therefore neither
hides order flow from the price nor makes the price overreact to it;
theory predicts that the FBA's price should react \emph{less} to
one-sided flow (\Cref{tab:theorylinks}), and the replay shows no such
mechanical effect. In the theory the reduction comes through behaviour,
liquidity providers quoting more aggressively once they are protected
from sniping, which a replay of fixed orders cannot reproduce. Within the same second the FBA's price moves more
(\ResCommonFlowLambdaFba\ against \ResCommonFlowLambdaCda, $p$
\ResCommonFlowLambdaP), but the gap comes only from the seconds with the
very largest flows, and the same-second value compares a VWAP with a
single clearing price over a sample in which a non-trading engine's
change is zero (\Cref{sec:formularq22}); the 5\,s value is the cleaner
comparison.
The CDA's per-sweep estimate, \ResKyleLambdaCda\,bps per SOL of a sweep's
executed quantity, is an average of per-second slopes; it describes the
CDA alone and is not on the scale of the pooled slopes above
(\Cref{sec:formularq22}).

\textbf{Price paths: the same price (F1).} The two engines' prices track each other closely
(\Cref{fig:pricepaths,fig:pricediff}). Over \PriceDiffN\ intervals in which
both traded, the FBA-minus-CDA VWAP difference has a median of
\PriceDiffMedianBps\,bps, a mean of \PriceDiffMeanBps\,bps and a standard
deviation of \PriceDiffStdBps\,bps. The gap widens only in fast markets: its
size correlates \PriceDiffVolCorr\ with CDA realized volatility, against
under 0.25 with either engine's trade count. The reason is mechanical. The
CDA's VWAP averages every fill as the book is swept within the second,
while the FBA clears once at one price, so in a fast market the two
definitions of ``the price of this second'' can differ by tens of bps
without either being wrong. A shorter batch interval would narrow this
gap, since the FBA would then clear several times within the second; only
$\tau = 1$\,s was run.

\textbf{Executed volume: the same (F1).} Volume is not statistically
different (\ResExecVolumeCda\ against \ResExecVolumeFba\,SOL per interval,
$p$ \ResExecVolumeP); over the month this is \$\SampleCdaNotional\ against
\$\SampleFbaNotional\ of notional (\Cref{tab:sample}). Both engines can
only trade the orders that were submitted, so the result means that
batching, although it delays trades, does not leave tradable flow
unmatched over the month.

\textbf{Trade count} is slightly higher for the FBA (\ResTradeCountFba\
against \ResTradeCountCda\ trades per interval, $p$ \ResTradeCountP). With
the same volume this means slightly smaller trades, consistent with one
clearing price splitting a large order among several counterparties.

\textbf{Time to execution: longer for the FBA (F5).} Waiting is the
clearest cost of batching:
\ResTimeToExecFba\,s for the FBA against \ResTimeToExecCda\,s for the CDA, a
gap of \ResTimeToExecDiff\,s ($p$ \ResTimeToExecP). Waiting for the next
clear explains at most one second of it, half a second on average for
orders arriving evenly within the interval. The rest is not explained by
the empty batches: \FbaEmptyBatchShare\% of one-second batches clear no
trade, but the CDA trades nothing in about the same share of seconds
(\Cref{sec:sample}), so the empty batches mostly mirror seconds in which
no order priced to cross arrived. The most likely source of the remainder
is which resting orders are matched once a batch does cross: the batch
fills the orders eligible at its clearing price in price-time order within
the batch, while the CDA fills an arriving order level by level from the
best resting price, so the two engines can fill different resting orders
from the same flow. This explanation is not measured directly here. A longer interval would add to the wait
for the clear, up to $\tau$, but would collect more orders per batch;
only $\tau = 1$\,s was run.

\textbf{Trader surplus} is marginally higher for the FBA
(\ResTraderSurplusFba\ against \ResTraderSurplusCda\,USD per interval, $p$
\ResTraderSurplusP). The difference does not come from uniform pricing as
such: on a trade between two limit orders, the buyer's and the seller's
improvement add up to the gap between their limits whatever the trade price
(\Cref{sec:priceimprovement}). It comes from a slightly different set of
matched orders, and from trades with a market order, whose side has no
limit and contributes nothing, so that only the limit side's improvement,
which does depend on the price, is counted (\Cref{sec:metrics23}).

\textbf{Engine controls} show whether each engine would keep up with the
order flow in real time; they do not bear on any other result
(\Cref{sec:limitsreplay}). The mean latency (\ResClearingLatencyCda\,$\mu$s
for the CDA against \ResClearingLatencyFba\,$\mu$s for the FBA) times one
order against one whole batch, which also re-evaluates every rolled-over
order, so its three-orders-of-magnitude gap reflects what is timed.
Throughput, which divides the same message count by computing time, is the
fairer comparison: \ResThroughputCda\ against \ResThroughputFba\ messages
per second of computing time, under four times apart. Both engines stay far
inside real-time needs: an FBA clear of about 2\,ms is three orders of
magnitude shorter than the one-second interval it has to fit into
(\Cref{fig:latencycontrol}).

\section{Conclusions from the Simulation}
\label{sec:res_summary}

\paragraph{Main conclusion.} When the same month of SOL order flow is
cleared in one-second batches instead of continuously, \emph{how much}
trades and \emph{at what price} stay the same (F1), the price responds to
the same net order flow by the same amount within five seconds (F2), and
uniform pricing removes price dispersion within a second (F3). Batching
adds no liquidity (F4) and delays execution (F5). The continuous market
charges resting orders a systematic adverse-selection cost (F6); whether
batching removes it remains open (\Cref{sec:limitssign}).

\subsection{How Each Finding Is Reached, Why It Arises, and How Far It
Can Be Relied On}
\label{sec:findingsreliability}

All findings rest on the same design: both engines receive exactly the
same orders, every metric is compared second by second as a paired
difference, and the standard errors allow for correlation between
consecutive seconds (\Cref{sec:experiments}). A difference between the
engines can therefore come only from the matching rule or from how a
metric is measured in each engine, and each finding below is checked for
both. With more than half a million paired seconds for most metrics, even
very small differences are statistically significant, so each finding is
judged on the size of the effect as well as on its $p$-value. For each
finding, \emph{Evidence} gives the numbers, \emph{Reasoning} how the
conclusion follows from them, \emph{Why} the mechanism that produces the
result, and \emph{Reliability} how far it can be relied on.
\Cref{tab:findings} summarises the result.

\begin{description}
  \item[F1 --- Same volume, same price.] \emph{Evidence:} executed volume
  of \ResExecVolumeCda\ against \ResExecVolumeFba\,SOL per second, with no
  significant difference ($p$ \ResExecVolumeP), and a VWAP gap with a
  median of \PriceDiffMedianBps\,bps and a standard deviation of
  \PriceDiffStdBps\,bps over \PriceDiffN\ seconds. \emph{Reasoning:} if
  batching left tradable flow unmatched, or moved the price away from the
  continuous one, the paired differences would show it; they do not.
  \emph{Why:} batching reschedules trades rather than preventing them. An
  order that would cross on arrival in the CDA waits for the next clear,
  and unfilled orders roll over instead of being dropped, so crossing
  interest still meets, at most one interval later. The price is set by
  the same limit prices in both engines. When an arriving order meets the
  resting orders, the CDA trades at each resting price in turn, while the
  FBA trades all of it at one price, the last level the order reaches. The
  two prices therefore coincide when an order takes one level and differ
  when it sweeps several, in either direction, which is why the gap has a
  median of zero and widens only in fast markets (\Cref{sec:res22}).
  \emph{Reliability: high.} Both engines can only trade the orders that
  were submitted, so the finding is that batching loses no volume, not
  that it creates any. Absolute volume describes the replay, which
  trades about twice the venue's volume, but this applies to both engines
  alike (\Cref{sec:limitsreplay}).

  \item[F2 --- Same price response to order flow.] \emph{Evidence:}
  Kyle's lambda at 5\,s of \ResCommonFlowLambdaFiveSCda\ against
  \ResCommonFlowLambdaFiveSFba\,bps per 1{,}000 SOL, with $p$
  \ResCommonFlowLambdaFiveSP\ for the difference, over
  $n = \ResCommonFlowLambdaFiveSNPaired$ seconds. \emph{Reasoning:} both
  engines are regressed on one regressor, the venue's own net taker flow,
  which is read from the venue's fill records and is produced by neither
  engine; the difference in slopes is tested directly
  (\Cref{sec:formularq22}). \emph{Why:} net buying removes the same
  resting sell orders in both engines, so once a burst of flow is over
  both prices sit at the same new level of the book; a few seconds later
  the two series have settled there. Within the same second the FBA's
  price moves further (\ResCommonFlowLambdaFba\ against
  \ResCommonFlowLambdaCda), for the reason given under F1: the clearing
  price jumps to the last level a large flow reaches, while the CDA's VWAP
  averages the levels on the way. The theory that predicts a smaller
  lambda under batching works through behaviour --- liquidity providers
  quoting more aggressively once protected from sniping --- which a replay
  of fixed orders cannot reproduce. \emph{Reliability: high at 5\,s.} The
  point estimates differ by under one percent, and a sample of this size
  would detect a small difference; formally the result is that no
  difference is found, not a proof of equality. The same-second
  comparison is weaker, because it sets a VWAP against a single clearing
  price.

  \item[F3 --- No price dispersion within a second.] \emph{Evidence:}
  dispersion of \ResDispersionCda\,bps in the CDA against
  \ResDispersionFba\,bps in the FBA, exactly zero in
  \DispersionFbaExactZeroPct\% of the seconds in which both traded.
  \emph{Reasoning:} every trade of a batch is at one price, and the batch
  interval equals the one-second measurement bucket, so a bucket holds at
  most one clear and its trades can have no dispersion. The seconds that
  are not exactly zero are those that received trades from two clears,
  because after a restart the batches no longer line up with the buckets
  (\Cref{sec:testresets}); they are a measurement effect, not a failure
  of uniform pricing. \emph{Why:} in the CDA, prices within one second
  differ because an order that sweeps the book trades at several levels,
  and because successive orders arrive at a book that has already moved.
  Uniform clearing replaces all of these prices with one. The consequence
  is that all traders who arrive within the same second receive the same
  price, whatever their order of arrival, which removes the reward for
  being first within the interval. The price variation does not disappear,
  however: it moves from within the second to between seconds, where it is
  measured as volatility (F4). \emph{Reliability: high.} The result
  follows from the rule itself and shows that the engine works as
  specified; it would not hold if the batch interval were shorter than the
  measurement bucket.

  \item[F4 --- No added liquidity at one second.] \emph{Evidence:} depth
  within 10, 50 and 100\,bps is lower for the FBA by between one and four
  percent ($p$ \ResDepthWithinTenBpsP); Amihud illiquidity is
  \ResAmihudFba\ against \ResAmihudCda\ ($p$ \ResAmihudP); daily
  volatility is \ResMatchedVolFba\ against \ResMatchedVolCda\,bps over
  \ResMatchedVolNPaired\ days ($p$ \ResMatchedVolP). \emph{Reasoning:}
  depth is the one measure defined the same way for both engines, and its
  only known bias favours the FBA (\Cref{sec:limitscomparable}); since
  the FBA is not deeper even so, batching did not add liquidity. The other
  two measures point the same way but are not like-for-like. Amihud
  illiquidity uses the book midpoint for the CDA and the clearing price for
  the FBA, and a clearing price moves between the bid and the ask side of
  the book with the direction of each batch even when the midpoint does
  not, which raises the FBA's value (\Cref{sec:res21}). Volatility compares
  a single clearing price with a VWAP that averages several fills and is
  therefore smoother (\Cref{sec:res22}). \emph{Why:} the argument for
  batching is that it pools orders that would otherwise arrive at
  different moments, so that each auction is deeper than the continuous
  book. At one second this market gives a batch little to pool: a batch
  that trades usually holds the same burst of crossing flow that the CDA
  trades in that second (F5), and the resting orders around it are the
  same orders in both engines. \emph{Reliability: high for the statement
  that batching adds no liquidity, low for the statement that it removes
  some.} The measured differences are small, partly due to measurement,
  and significant mainly because of the sample size. The finding holds for
  $\tau = 1$\,s only.

  \item[F5 --- Batching delays execution.] \emph{Evidence:} time to first
  fill of \ResTimeToExecFba\,s against \ResTimeToExecCda\,s ($p$
  \ResTimeToExecP); \FbaEmptyBatchShare\% of batches clear no trade, while
  the CDA trades in about a quarter of all seconds as well
  (\Cref{sec:res22}), and the two engines trade in largely the same
  seconds. \emph{Reasoning:} the delay is significant and in the
  direction batching implies. The empty batches do not explain it, because
  the CDA has as many seconds without a trade; they show instead that in
  most seconds no order arrives that is priced to cross. \emph{Why:} part
  of the delay is the wait for the clear, at most one second and half a
  second on average for orders arriving evenly. The rest most likely comes
  from which resting orders are matched once a batch crosses: the batch
  fills the orders eligible at its single price in price-time order within
  the batch, while the CDA fills an arriving order level by level, so the
  two engines can fill different resting orders from the same flow. This
  explanation is not measured directly. \emph{Reliability: high for the
  direction, lower for the size.} A fill that comes long after an order's
  submission falls outside the window the metric is computed on and is not
  counted, for either engine; because FBA orders wait longer, this more
  likely understates the gap than overstates it.

  \item[F6 --- Resting orders pay an adverse-selection cost in the CDA.]
  \emph{Evidence:} effective spread \ResEffectiveSpreadCda\,bps, realized
  spread \ResRealizedSpreadOneSCda/\ResRealizedSpreadFiveSCda/\ResRealizedSpreadThirtySCda\,bps
  and price impact
  \ResPriceImpactOneSCda/\ResPriceImpactFiveSCda/\ResPriceImpactThirtySCda\,bps
  at 1/5/30\,s. \emph{Reasoning:} each CDA trade has an observed direction,
  and a negative realized spread with a price impact that grows with the
  horizon is the standard signature of adverse selection
  (\Cref{sec:spread-decomposition}). \emph{Why:} a resting order is a
  standing offer that anyone can accept, and it is accepted most often by
  traders who expect the price to move in their favour. The maker earns
  the spread at the moment of the trade, but the price then moves against
  it by more than that, so on average it loses. Hyperliquid's processing of
  cancellations before new orders (\Cref{sec:venue}) protects quotes
  against being picked off within a block, but not against better-informed
  traders. \emph{Reliability: high for the pattern,} which holds at all
  three horizons. The magnitudes describe the replay, in which part of the
  volume comes from post-only orders that would not have traded as takers
  on the venue (\Cref{sec:testfidelity}). It is a finding about the CDA
  only; no FBA counterpart can be compared (\Cref{sec:limitssign}).
\end{description}

Trader surplus (\ResTraderSurplusCda\ against \ResTraderSurplusFba\,USD
per second) is not listed as a finding: it barely changes, as the transfer
argument of \Cref{sec:priceimprovement} predicts, and so confirms the
theory rather than adding to it.

\paragraph{Interpretation.} Taken together, the findings say that at one
second, on this market, batching largely reproduces the continuous market
with a short delay. The same orders trade at the same prices (F1), the
price absorbs order flow in the same way (F2), and the only change in
how prices are formed is the one the rule guarantees: one price per
batch instead of several per second (F3). The gains batching promises
depend on pooling, gathering orders from both sides that would otherwise
arrive at different moments, and at one second a batch rarely holds more
than the flow that would have traded anyway, so no gain in liquidity
appears (F4). What remains is the cost of batching, a longer wait for
execution (F5). Whether a longer interval would give the batches enough
flow to pool is the main open question this leaves.

\paragraph{What remains open.} The simulation does not settle whether
batching reduces adverse selection (\Cref{sec:limitssign}), how
participants would behave under batching (\Cref{sec:limitsbehaviour}),
or whether a longer interval would fill the batches, since only
$\tau = 1$\,s was run.

\section{Limits of the Comparison}
\label{sec:metricreliability}

This section sets out the limits on comparing the metrics across the two
engines: the missing trade direction of the FBA, the metrics that cannot
be compared or are compared only with a caveat, and the fidelity of the
replay. \Cref{tab:confidence} closes with how far each metric family can
be relied on. Limits of the research design as a whole are discussed in
\Cref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{The FBA's Trades Have No Direction}
\label{sec:limitssign}

FBA trades have no direction (\Cref{sec:fbavscda}), so the FBA spread
values are an estimated sign, from a tick rule on the CDA's prices, times
the unsigned deviation the simulator records (\Cref{sec:metrics21}); the
sign exists in \SignedFbaSpreadNSigned\ of the \SignedFbaSpreadNMatched\
buckets in which both engines traded. Two points make these values
unusable as a cost measure. First, the formulas need the sign times the
\emph{signed} deviation $p_k - m_k$. Because both engines' prices move
together, the CDA's tick largely follows the FBA's own price move, so the
sign times $|p_k - m_k|$ reduces to the plain price change, whose average is
the price drift; the near-zero values would appear whatever the FBA's cost.
Second, all three FBA measures apply the same sign to the same trades, so
their agreement is one result, not three.

The CDA side needs no estimate and stands. The FBA side is reported for
completeness but not compared, and no conclusion is drawn from it about
whether batching lowers the adverse-selection cost. Measuring that would
need the simulator to record the signed deviation of each FBA trade and a
direction that does not come from a price, such as the sign of the venue's
net taker flow in that second (\Cref{sec:formularq22}).

\subsection{Which Metrics Can Be Compared}
\label{sec:limitscomparable}

Most metrics are defined the same way for both engines and are compared
directly: executed volume, trade count and VWAP, depth within a
threshold, intra-interval dispersion, time to execution and trader
surplus. Three metrics cannot be compared at all, and five are compared
with a caveat.

\paragraph{Not comparable.} \textbf{Depth at best} compares one CDA price
level with the whole eligible side of the FBA schedule at the clearing
price, $(D(\clr) + S(\clr))/2$, which adds up all orders priced at or
better than $\clr$. The FBA's higher value is a consequence of this
definition, not of more liquidity, so the two are reported but not
compared. \textbf{Realized volatility within a bucket} needs two prices in
one second, which the FBA almost never has; matched-timestamp volatility
replaces it. \textbf{The spread decomposition} needs a trade direction
the FBA does not have (\Cref{sec:limitssign}).

\paragraph{Compared with a caveat.} \textbf{Depth within a threshold}
carries the depth result. Its one asymmetry, that the FBA's value is read
just before the batch clears and so includes orders about to execute, can
only make the FBA look deeper, so it does not explain the finding that
the FBA is thinner. \textbf{Quoted spread} is computed correctly for both
engines, but the FBA's is read from the leftover orders just after a
clear, which is not the same kind of object as a CDA book that has
continuously matched everything it could (\Cref{sec:fbavscda,app:comparability}).
It is therefore weaker evidence about liquidity than depth within a
threshold. \textbf{Amihud illiquidity} uses each engine's own reference
price, the midpoint for the CDA and the clearing price for the FBA; the
clearing price moves between the bid and the ask side with the direction
of each batch, which raises the FBA's value (\Cref{sec:res21}).
\textbf{Matched-timestamp volatility} compares the CDA's VWAP, an average
of several fills, with the FBA's single clearing price, which is less
smooth (\Cref{sec:res22}). \textbf{Kyle's lambda} regresses both engines on the same
regressor, the venue's own net taker flow per second
(\Cref{sec:formularq22}), so the two slopes are directly comparable. Two
limits remain. The regressor is the flow that executed on the venue, not
on either simulated engine, and in the FBA the same orders may trade in a
different second or not at all. And the same-second slope compares a VWAP
with a single clearing price (\Cref{sec:res22}); the 5\,s horizon avoids
this.

\subsection{Fidelity of the Replay}
\label{sec:limitsreplay}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Volume level.} The replay does not enforce post-only orders
  or self-trade prevention, so it trades about twice the venue's recorded
  volume (\Cref{sec:testfidelity}). Both engines receive this same input, so
  it does not favour either side of a paired comparison, but absolute
  volume, trade-count and latency levels describe the replay, not the live
  market.
  \item \textbf{Rounding.} Sizes are rounded to whole SOL, and orders below
  0.5\,SOL are skipped for both engines (\Cref{sec:dataset}).
  \item \textbf{Restarts.} The month was replayed with \VerResetN\
  restarts in its first five days, which emptied both engines at the same
  record. From the second of them onward the FBA's batches no longer line
  up exactly with the one-second buckets, so a bucket can hold trades from
  two batch clears (\Cref{sec:testresets}).
  \item \textbf{Implementation.} A difference could in principle come from
  how well each engine is implemented. This is checked by the shared order
  representation and metrics code, the independent reference implementation
  and the test suites (\Cref{app:testing}); convergence of the FBA to the
  CDA as $\tau \to 0$, the strongest such check, was not run.
  \item \textbf{Engine controls.} The replay runs on the data's timestamps,
  so computing speed cannot change any market metric. Throughput and
  latency are wall-clock measurements and the only two of the 35 output
  columns that are not reproduced bit for bit across runs
  (\Cref{sec:testdeterminism}).
\end{itemize}

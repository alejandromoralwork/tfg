\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
\markboth{Abstract}{Abstract}

Continuous limit order books process orders serially, in the sequence in
which they arrive. This single architectural property creates a
mechanical incentive to compete on speed rather than on price, and the
resulting latency arms race imposes a measurable cost on liquidity
demanders. The frequent batch auction (FBA) has been proposed as a
market design response: orders are collected over a short discrete
interval and cleared simultaneously at a single uniform price, which
removes the value of a marginal speed advantage inside the interval.

This thesis studies the FBA from two directions. The theoretical part
decomposes an FBA matching engine into five design blocks --- the batch
interval, the information disclosure regime, order flow segregation,
the matching and rationing rule, and settlement and residual handling
---  it enumerates the options available for each block, and derives from
auction theory and market microstructure theory which strategic
behaviour each option induces. The result is a structured design space
and a trade-off matrix that makes explicit which incentive an exchange
operator buys and which one it sells with every architectural choice.

The empirical part turns that design space into code. Two matching
engines, a continuous double auction and a frequent batch auction, are
implemented in Rust behind one shared matching interface and driven by
the same replayed Level~4 order flow from the Hyperliquid exchange for the token Solana (SOL). The comparison covers liquidity and transaction costs,price discovery and market quality, and execution, allocation and
order-arrival timing.

\vspace{0.6em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} market microstructure; frequent batch
auction; matching engine; latency arbitrage; uniform-price clearing;
Level~4 order book data.
